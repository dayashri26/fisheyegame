Who Was the Greatest Archer in the Mahabharata?

Arjuna, Karna, Eklavya — the epic gives each of them a legitimate claim, and refuses to pick a winner.

Ask a hundred readers of the Mahabharata who the greatest archer in the epic was, and you will get at least three confident, mutually exclusive answers. Some will say Arjuna, without hesitation — the disciplined prince who strung the bow no one else could lift and put an arrow through the eye of a spinning fish by watching only its reflection. Others will say Karna, the outsider whose raw talent rivaled Arjuna's despite a lifetime of being denied the training that shaped him. A smaller but no less passionate camp will say Eklavya, the tribal boy who taught himself archery in a forest and, by his own guru's later admission, may have surpassed them both. The truth is that the question of who was the greatest archer in the Mahabharata does not have a single, textually settled answer — and that ambiguity is not a flaw in the story. It is one of the reasons the epic has stayed alive for thousands of years.

The case for Arjuna

Arjuna's claim rests on the widest base of evidence in the text. He is trained from boyhood by Dronacharya, the era's foremost teacher of archery, and by every account becomes his most gifted pupil — the one who could nock, aim, and release arrows in near-total darkness, and who once demonstrated such single-minded focus during a target drill that he saw nothing in the world but the bird's eye he was told to hit. His skill is validated again and again across the epic: he wins Draupadi's hand by performing the Matsya Vedh, the legendary fish-eye trial, when kings and warriors before him had failed even to string the bow; he receives celestial weapons directly from gods including Indra and Shiva after arduous penance; and during the eighteen days of the Kurukshetra war, he fights and survives duels against nearly every major warrior on the opposing side, including Karna himself. Arjuna's greatness is not just raw ability — it is ability compounded by relentless discipline, formal training, and divine favor, which is precisely why so many readers default to naming him the greatest archer in the Mahabharata without a second thought.

The case for Karna

Karna complicates that answer immediately. Born to Kunti before her marriage and raised by a charioteer's family, he is denied formal training under Drona because of his uncertain birth, and instead learns from the sage Parashurama by concealing his true identity — a deception that costs him a curse the moment it is discovered. Despite that disadvantage, Karna's natural talent is treated by the epic as a genuine match for Arjuna's trained skill. He arrives uninvited at the archery tournament where Arjuna first displays his abilities and matches every feat performed there, is prevented from a duel only by questions about his lineage, and later becomes a formidable commander for the Kaurava side specifically because his archery is considered a real threat to Arjuna's. What makes Karna's case so compelling to many readers is less a question of technical skill than of circumstance: he achieves what he achieves without the mentorship, resources, or fair treatment Arjuna receives, and the epic frames his tragedy — curses, betrayals, and a battlefield death arriving through misfortune as much as swordplay — as central to its emotional core. If greatness is measured against obstacles overcome, Karna's claim to being the best archer in the Mahabharata is very hard to dismiss.

Eklavya: the archer the epic almost erases

And then there is Eklavya, whose story unsettles both of the previous arguments. A prince of the Nishada tribal community, Eklavya is refused instruction by Drona, who has already committed to teaching only the Kuru princes. Undeterred, Eklavya builds a clay image of Drona in the forest, treats it as his guru, and through sheer self-discipline becomes an archer so skilled that Drona, encountering him by chance, considers him a threat to Arjuna's claim as his finest pupil. Drona then asks Eklavya for guru-dakshina — a customary offering to one's teacher — and demands his right thumb, permanently ending his archery career. Many readers consider this the most quietly devastating episode in the entire epic, and it raises a genuinely uncomfortable question: if a self-taught archer had already matched or exceeded Arjuna before that request, was Eklavya, for a time, the greatest archer in the Mahabharata in pure ability — regardless of what the political and social structure of the story allowed him to become afterward?

The elder generation: Bhishma and Drona

It's worth pausing on two more names before reaching any conclusion, because the epic itself does. Bhishma, the grand-patriarch of the Kuru line, is a warrior of near-mythic capability who had received his own weapons training from Parashurama and remains, even in extreme old age, formidable enough that it takes a coordinated and ethically fraught maneuver during the war to bring him down. Drona, the teacher of both Arjuna and Karna, is himself described as one of the finest archers of his generation, having trained under the same legendary source and later passing that mastery on. Neither is typically nominated as "the" greatest archer by most readers, since both belong to an older generation whose peak the story largely tells us about rather than shows us directly in youth. But their presence matters: they are the standard against which Arjuna and Karna are measured, and the fact that the epic treats them with such reverence is part of why a title as good as either of their students earns so much weight.

Why the epic never answers the question

What's striking, reading the Mahabharata as a whole, is how carefully it avoids resolving this debate. The text gives Arjuna the most victories and the clearest divine endorsement, gives Karna the most sympathetic tragedy and an equally matched skill set, and gives Eklavya a moment of unmatched raw talent immediately followed by its own undoing at the hands of the social order the story otherwise upholds. That refusal to settle the matter isn't an oversight — sprawling epics built over centuries of oral and written transmission tend to hold multiple truths at once, especially about characters whose fates carry moral weight. The Mahabharata isn't only interested in who could shoot straightest; it's interested in what talent costs, who gets to develop it, and what happens to the people the system was never built to reward. That is arguably the more honest reason there is no single greatest archer in the Mahabharata — the question was never purely about archery to begin with.

So who was the greatest archer in the Mahabharata? The honest answer is that the epic hands you three strong candidates and asks you to sit with the discomfort of not being able to fully rank them. Arjuna earns the title through discipline, training, and a career of proven victories. Karna earns it through talent that flourished despite every disadvantage stacked against him. Eklavya earns it, at least for a moment, through a devotion to the craft that no privilege or mentorship could match — before the story takes even that away from him. Rather than diminishing the epic, that unresolved rivalry is part of why readers keep returning to it: greatness, the Mahabharata suggests, was never going to be simple enough to fit on a leaderboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is considered the greatest archer in the Mahabharata? The epic does not settle on one name. Arjuna has the widest evidence — formal training, the fish-eye trial, celestial weapons, and duels won across the war — while Karna and Eklavya both have strong, differently-argued claims.

Was Eklavya more skilled than Arjuna? The text suggests he may have been, at least briefly: Drona himself considered Eklavya's self-taught skill a threat to Arjuna's standing as his finest pupil, which is why he demanded Eklavya's thumb as guru-dakshina to end his archery career.

Does the epic ever declare a definitive greatest archer? No. The Mahabharata gives Arjuna the most victories, Karna the most sympathetic and equally matched skill, and Eklavya a moment of unmatched raw talent — and deliberately leaves the rivalry unresolved rather than crowning one of them.

How does Karna compare to Arjuna? Karna is treated by the epic as a genuine technical match for Arjuna despite being denied the formal training and mentorship Arjuna received, matching his feats at the tournament where Arjuna first displayed his skill and later becoming a threat the Kaurava side relied on specifically because of that parity.
