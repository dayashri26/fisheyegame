Ancient Indian Archery Weapons: A Field Guide From the Epics

Gandiva, Vijaya, Pinaka, and the astra-shastra divide — a grounded walk through what the Mahabharata actually says about its bows and arrows.

Ancient Indian archery weapons occupy a strange middle ground in popular imagination — half battlefield equipment, half myth. Open the Mahabharata or the Ramayana and you find bows with names, arrows that behave like something between projectile and prayer, and a vocabulary of weaponry that doesn't map cleanly onto anything in modern military history. None of that is a flaw in the storytelling. It's the point. This is a field guide to the actual archery-related weapons and terms that appear in these texts and in the broader Dhanurveda tradition of Indian martial lore — what they were said to be, who wielded them, and where legend is doing the talking rather than history.

Astra and shastra: the difference that matters most

Before naming a single bow, it helps to understand a distinction the epics themselves draw carefully: the difference between an astra and a shastra. A shastra, in this tradition, is simply a weapon used by hand or by direct physical action — a sword, a mace, an ordinary arrow loosed the way any archer looses an arrow. An astra, by contrast, is a weapon that has been empowered or "invoked" through a mantra, a specific invocation taught by a teacher or granted by a deity. Once invoked, an astra is described in the texts as behaving less like a physical object and more like a directed force — capable of effects no ordinary arrow could produce, and, in many episodes, requiring a counter-astra to be neutralized rather than simply dodged or blocked.

This astra-shastra distinction sits at the heart of how the Mahabharata talks about archery weapons throughout the epic. A warrior might carry dozens of shastras — plain arrows, for volume and everyday combat — while possessing only a handful of astras, each tied to a specific teacher, a specific discipline of restraint, and often a specific warning about the danger of using it carelessly. It's worth being clear that astras are presented in the tradition as legendary and celestial in nature, not as historical battlefield technology; the epics themselves frame them this way, as extraordinary rather than ordinary.

Named bows of the epics

Bows, more than any other equipment, are where the Mahabharata gives individual weapons real identity. The most famous is Gandiva, the bow of Arjuna, described in the epic as a divine bow gifted to him by the fire god Agni — acquired, in the story, during the burning of the Khandava forest, alongside an inexhaustible quiver. Gandiva functions almost as a character in its own right across the epic: its twang is described as distinct enough to be recognized from a distance, and its loss or temporary absence in certain episodes is treated as narratively significant, a sign that something has shifted for Arjuna himself.

Karna, Arjuna's great rival and half-brother, wields his own named bow, Vijaya, meaning "victory." Vijaya is described in the tradition as a bow of similarly formidable make, associated in some tellings with the sage Parashurama, under whom Karna trained. The pairing of Gandiva and Vijaya across the Kurukshetra war narrative — two named, storied bows in the hands of two of the epic's greatest archers — is part of what gives their eventual confrontation its weight in the text.

Two other bows are worth including for context, even though they belong to different corners of the tradition. Rama's bow in the Ramayana is not always given a proper name in the way Gandiva is, but it plays a comparably central role — most famously in the episode of Sita's swayamvara, where suitors are asked to string an immense bow associated with Shiva, and Rama alone succeeds, in the process breaking it. That bow, generally identified with Pinaka, Shiva's great bow, also appears in Mahabharata-adjacent tradition connected to Draupadi's own swayamvara lore in some retellings, where a bow of extraordinary weight and difficulty is set as the trial's central obstacle before Arjuna performs the feat the game draws its name from. Traditions vary in exactly how these bow-legends connect across the two epics, and it's fair to say plainly that the details here are legendary rather than historically documented — these are stories about divine and semi-divine weapons, not equipment inventories.

Arrows, quivers, and the astras by name

Where bows in the epics are individual and named, arrows tend to be described more by category and function. Ordinary arrows — shastra-class, in the terms above — are described simply as sharpened, feathered shafts loosed in quantity, the everyday currency of epic battle scenes. Quivers, meanwhile, get their own moment of legend: Arjuna's quivers, received alongside Gandiva, are described as inexhaustible, never running empty regardless of how many arrows are drawn from them across a day of fighting. It's a detail worth noting as textual color rather than a claim about any real archery equipment — the epic is telling you something about the scale of the gift, not describing a technology.

Named astras are more numerous than named bows, and the Mahabharata and related texts mention many of them — among the better documented are weapons associated with fire, wind, and other elemental or divine forces, each invoked through mantra and each governed by a code of restraint that warriors are repeatedly warned not to violate. The specific effects attributed to any given astra vary somewhat between tellings and later retellings, and this article won't attempt to catalogue exact powers weapon by weapon, since that's exactly the territory where popular retellings start inventing detail the source texts don't actually support. What's consistently true across the tradition is the underlying idea: an astra is a weapon of knowledge as much as of iron, and losing the discipline to control it is treated in the epic as at least as dangerous as facing one.

Taken together, these weapons — Gandiva and Vijaya as named bows, Pinaka as the great bow of the Shiva tradition, the astra-shastra distinction governing everything from ordinary arrows to celestial ones, and the inexhaustible quivers that turn a gift into a legend — form the real, textually grounded core of what ancient Indian archery weapons meant within the epics. They were never meant to read as a technical manual. They were meant to tell you something about the archer holding them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an astra and a shastra? A shastra is an ordinary weapon used by direct physical action, such as a hand-loosed arrow or sword, while an astra is a weapon empowered through a specific mantra taught by a teacher or granted by a deity, described in the epics as behaving like a directed force rather than a simple projectile.

What was Arjuna's bow called? Arjuna's bow was Gandiva, a divine bow said to have been gifted to him by the fire god Agni during the burning of the Khandava forest, along with an inexhaustible quiver of arrows.

What was Karna's bow called? Karna's bow was named Vijaya, meaning “victory,” a formidable bow associated in some tellings with the sage Parashurama, under whom Karna trained.
