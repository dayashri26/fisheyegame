Dussehra and the Tradition of Ceremonial Archery in India

Long before it meant fireworks and towering effigies, Dussehra carried a quieter custom: the ceremonial honoring of the bow.

Every autumn, as Dussehra approaches, the festival is best known across much of northern India for its towering effigies of Ravana going up in flame — a spectacle built on the Ramayana's account of Rama's victory. But sit with the dussehra archery tradition a little longer and a quieter, older thread comes into view: a set of customs, observed differently in different regions, that tie this turning point of the autumn calendar to the honoring of weapons, and above all, of the bow. It is not one single nationwide story so much as a layered inheritance — regional practices that developed along parallel lines, sharing a season and a spirit of triumph without ever quite merging into a single script.

Start with what actually links archery to this date on the calendar. Dussehra falls on Vijayadashami, the tenth day that closes the nine nights of Navratri, and in many communities that closing day carries with it a custom called Ayudha Puja — the worship of tools and weapons. Farmers set down their ploughs to have them blessed. Artisans lay out their instruments. Soldiers, and in earlier centuries kings and their armies, brought forward swords, shields, and bows to be consecrated before the year's work or campaigns resumed. This is not an archery festival in the narrow sense — it is a working culture's way of pausing to honor the implements by which it earns its living and defends itself, and the bow, as the preeminent weapon of the Indian epic imagination, sat naturally at the center of that reverence wherever the custom took hold.

The Pandavas and the Shami Tree

One of the most evocative threads in this dussehra archery tradition comes not from the Ramayana at all, but from the Mahabharata. During their thirteenth and final year of exile, the five Pandava brothers were bound by the terms of their banishment to live in disguise, undetected, in the kingdom of Virata. Weapons — Arjuna's great bow Gandiva chief among them — would have given them away instantly. So before entering the city, tradition holds that they wrapped their bows, arrows, and swords in cloth and concealed them high in the branches of a shami tree, a hardy, thorny tree long associated in Indian tradition with strength and quiet endurance. There the weapons waited, untouched, through a year of hidden identities and quiet tests of patience, until the year of exile ended and the Pandavas could climb back up, retrieve Gandiva and its companions, and reclaim who they were before the climactic war of Kurukshetra.

In parts of India — Maharashtra and Karnataka among them — this episode is folded into Vijayadashami through the custom of shami puja, in which the shami tree itself is worshipped and its leaves exchanged as tokens of goodwill, drawing a line from the Pandavas' retrieved weapons to the day's larger theme of setting out, renewed, after a period of restraint. It is worth being precise here: this is a regional association, carried with real weight in some communities and largely unknown in others, rather than a single pan-Indian explanation for why Dussehra exists. India's festival calendar rarely resolves into one tidy origin story, and Dussehra is a particularly good example of why — it sits at the crossing point of at least two great epics and a wide range of local harvest and martial customs, all reaching the same date from different directions.

One Festival, Several Victories

That crossing point is worth dwelling on. In the Hindi-speaking north, Dussehra is told chiefly through the Ramayana — the day Rama's arrow finally ended Ravana's reign of ten heads and immense power, a victory of dharma reenacted every year in the Ramlila performances that precede the effigy burning. In Bengal and much of the east, the same ten days are understood primarily through Durga Puja, and Vijayadashima marks the day the goddess Durga, armed by the gods with a trident, discus, and bow among her weapons, defeated the buffalo-demon Mahishasura. These are not variants of one master narrative competing for the "true" version — they are distinct traditions, rooted in different scriptures and different regional histories, that happen to converge on the same lunar day and share a common grammar: the armed triumph of order over chaos, marked at the changing of a season. The Ayudha Puja weapon-worship and the shami tree custom sit alongside both of these, sometimes overlapping with one, sometimes the other, depending on where in India you are standing.

What unites all of these regional expressions, whatever their scripture of origin, is the recurring image of the bow as the instrument of a rightful victory — wielded by a divine king, a goddess, or a band of exiled princes reclaiming their due. It is not a coincidence that a festival marking triumph so often reaches for archery as its symbol. In the world of the epics, the bow was never merely a weapon; it was a test of character, discipline, and inner steadiness, and the warrior worthy of it had to prove all three before the string was ever drawn.

Dhanurveda and the Discipline Behind the Bow

That idea of the bow as a discipline rather than just a tool has its own long lineage in Dhanurveda, the ancient Indian science of archery and martial training referenced across the epics and later classical texts. Dhanurveda treated the bow as inseparable from the mind that drew it — instruction is described as covering stance, breath, and concentration alongside technique, on the understanding that a wavering mind produced a wavering shot long before a wavering hand did. Ceremonial customs like Ayudha Puja and the reverence shown to the shami-tree weapons are, in a sense, a folk continuation of that same worldview: the weapon is not treated as inert metal and wood, but as something that carries the discipline, focus, and character of the person who wields it, and so deserves to be honored in its own right at the turning of the year.

It is that same thread — focus as the true test of the archer, more than raw strength — that runs straight through to the most famous archery trial in the Mahabharata: Matsya Vedh, the piercing of the fish's eye at Draupadi's swayamvara, where the successful suitor had to strike a spinning target by watching only its reflection in the water below. That trial did not test who could pull the heaviest bow. It tested who could hold their attention steady while everything else moved. It is the same steadiness the epics keep returning to whenever a bow is raised, whether in Arjuna's hands, in Rama's, or in Durga's own.

None of this is to flatten Dussehra into a single tidy meaning. It is, depending on where you live in India, a Ramayana festival, a Durga Puja festival, a harvest festival, a soldiers' and craftsmen's festival of tools, and in some households, a quiet nod to two brothers climbing a shami tree to reclaim what they had hidden. The archery running through so many of these threads is not an accident of storytelling — it reflects a culture that, across very different regions and very different scriptures, kept reaching for the same image to describe a hard-won, disciplined triumph.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the connection between Dussehra and archery? Dussehra falls on Vijayadashami, a day tied in many regions to Ayudha Puja, the ceremonial worship of tools and weapons, and to the Mahabharata's shami tree episode — both traditions place the bow at the center of the festival's theme of righteous, disciplined victory.

What is Ayudha Puja? Ayudha Puja is a Vijayadashami custom in which farmers, artisans, soldiers, and others have their tools and weapons — the bow prominent among them historically — blessed before resuming their work or campaigns for the new year.

Why do the Pandavas hide their weapons in a shami tree? During their final year of exile the Pandavas had to live undetected in the kingdom of Virata, so they concealed their weapons, including Arjuna's bow Gandiva, in a shami tree, retrieving them only once their year of disguise ended.

Does every region in India celebrate Dussehra the same way? No — the north largely tells Dussehra through the Ramayana and Rama's defeat of Ravana, the east through Durga Puja and the goddess's victory over Mahishasura, and other regions add customs like shami puja, so the festival reflects several distinct traditions converging on one date rather than a single national story.
