Adi Parva Summary: Where the Mahabharata Begins

Everything that happens before the bow is even strung — the births, the rivalries, and the narrow escape that set up the fish-eye trial.

Anyone looking for a straightforward adi parva summary usually has one honest question underneath it: where do I actually start with the Mahabharata? The epic is vast — eighteen parvas, or books, containing roughly a hundred thousand verses — and wading in at a random chapter rarely works. The Adi Parva, whose name simply means "book of beginnings," is the epic's answer to that problem. It is the first of the eighteen parvas, and its entire job is to get every major character, relationship, and grudge into position before the real conflict begins.

The parva opens with a frame that the Mahabharata returns to again and again: a story within a story. The sage Vaishampayana, a disciple of Vyasa, narrates the epic to King Janamejaya during a great snake sacrifice, while Vyasa himself is credited as the poem's original composer. This nested structure — a bard telling a king a story that was told to him by a sage — is one of the epic's most distinctive features, and it's established right here at the outset, before a single Kaurava or Pandava is even born.

Births and childhoods: the Kauravas and Pandavas

Once the frame is set, the Adi Parva moves quickly through genealogy. King Shantanu of Hastinapura's line eventually passes to two brothers, Dhritarashtra and Pandu — Dhritarashtra born blind, Pandu fair-skinned but under a curse that will prevent him from safely fathering children. Dhritarashtra marries Gandhari, who bears him a hundred sons, the Kauravas, the eldest of whom is Duryodhana. Pandu, unable to conceive naturally because of his curse, has five sons instead through his wives Kunti and Madri, with the help of invoked gods — Yudhishthira, Bhima, and Arjuna born to Kunti, and the twins Nakula and Sahadeva born to Madri. These five are the Pandavas, and after Pandu's early death, all the cousins are raised together in Hastinapura under Dhritarashtra's household.

Even as boys, the two sets of cousins pull in opposite directions. Bhima's raw strength and Duryodhana's simmering resentment clash constantly, and Duryodhana — anxious that Yudhishthira, as the eldest of the group, has a stronger claim to the throne — begins scheming against the Pandavas almost from childhood. This rivalry, seeded early in the Adi Parva, is the thread that the rest of the Mahabharata eventually pulls taut into open war.

Training under Dronacharya

The princes' formal education comes from the master archer and strategist Dronacharya, appointed as their royal teacher. Under Drona, Arjuna distinguishes himself as the most gifted archer among all the cousins, a detail the epic plants deliberately — it pays off directly later, at the fish-eye trial. This section of the Adi Parva also introduces Karna, who trains separately and later becomes both a formidable rival to Arjuna and one of the epic's most tragic figures, and it includes the famous public tournament where the young princes demonstrate their skills before the court, further sharpening the sense of competition between the two branches of the family.

Drona eventually asks his students for a form of tuition: to help him defeat King Drupada of Panchala, an old rival, and seize half his kingdom. The Pandavas succeed where the Kauravas fail, humiliating Drupada — a defeat he never forgets, and one that quietly sets up his motivation to seek Arjuna specifically as a son-in-law once his daughter comes of age.

The lacquer house and the escape into hiding

As the Pandavas grow into young men and their popularity with the people of Hastinapura rises, Duryodhana's jealousy hardens into an actual plot. With his uncle Shakuni and brother Dushasana, he arranges for the Pandavas and their mother Kunti to be sent to the town of Varanavata and lodged in a specially constructed house — the infamous lacquer house, or Jatugriha — built from flammable, resin-soaked materials and designed to burn to the ground with the family inside.

The plan nearly works. But the Pandavas are warned in advance, most accounts crediting their uncle Vidura with the tip-off, and they have an escape tunnel dug beneath the house before the fire is set. When the lacquer house burns, Hastinapura believes the Pandavas and Kunti have died in the blaze, while in reality the family slips away and goes into hiding, disguised as wandering Brahmins. This is the turning point of the Adi Parva: the Pandavas spend an extended period effectively erased from the political map, moving quietly through the forest and nearby settlements while their cousins believe the succession question has been permanently resolved in Duryodhana's favor.

It's during this stretch in hiding that some of the parva's other well-known episodes occur, including Bhima's encounter with the demon Hidimba and his marriage to Hidimba's sister, Hidimbi. But the Pandavas' disguise and the fiction of their death also serve a narrative purpose: it lets them re-enter public life later at a moment of their own choosing, rather than as reigning princes constantly watched by a hostile court.

Draupadi's swayamvara and the fish-eye trial

That moment of re-entry is exactly where the Adi Parva builds to its climax. Word reaches the disguised Pandavas of a swayamvara — a self-choice marriage ceremony — being held by King Drupada of Panchala for his daughter Draupadi. Drupada, still smarting from his earlier defeat at the hands of Drona's students, has designed the contest's central test with Arjuna specifically in mind, though he keeps this hope private: a heavy bow must be strung, and five arrows shot through a small, rotating target shaped like a fish, aiming only at the fish's reflection in a pool of water below rather than at the fish itself.

Kings, princes, and warriors from across the land attempt the trial and fail, unable even to lift the bow. Karna steps forward and very nearly succeeds, but is turned away in most tellings on a point of lineage before he can loose his shot. Then Arjuna, still disguised as a Brahmin among the gathered crowd, comes forward, strings the bow, and pierces the target clean through — winning Draupadi's hand and, in doing so, revealing the Pandavas' survival to the entire assembled court. The Adi Parva doesn't quite end at the swayamvara itself — it carries on into the aftermath, including the unusual circumstance of Draupadi becoming the shared wife of all five Pandava brothers, and the family's eventual return to Hastinapura and formal division of the kingdom. But the swayamvara is the parva's emotional turning point: the moment the Pandavas stop being fugitives and start being contenders again, in full view of the cousins who thought they were dead.

Read as a whole, the Adi Parva earns its name. It is patient in a way modern retellings sometimes aren't, spending real time on lineage, education, and slow-building resentment before it ever gets to a dramatic set piece. But that patience is exactly what makes the fish-eye trial land the way it does — by the time Arjuna picks up that bow, the reader already knows precisely what has been lost, what is being risked, and why hitting a reflection instead of a target is the perfect final test for a family that has spent the whole parva learning to survive by not being seen directly at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Adi Parva? The Adi Parva, meaning “book of beginnings,” is the first of the Mahabharata's eighteen parvas, introducing the epic's narrative frame along with the births, childhoods, and rivalries of the Kauravas and Pandavas.

What happens in the Adi Parva? It covers the births and training of the Kauravas and Pandavas under Dronacharya, Duryodhana's plot to burn the Pandavas alive in the lacquer house, their escape into hiding, and their eventual re-emergence at Draupadi's swayamvara, where Arjuna wins the fish-eye trial.

How many parvas does the Mahabharata have? The Mahabharata is traditionally divided into eighteen parvas, or books, containing roughly a hundred thousand verses in total.

Where does the Adi Parva end? The Adi Parva continues past the swayamvara itself into its immediate aftermath, including Draupadi becoming the shared wife of all five Pandava brothers and the family's return to Hastinapura for the formal division of the kingdom.
